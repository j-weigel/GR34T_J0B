Decoding the Visual Brain

Can you tell what someone is looking at from their brain activity alone? A machine-learning analysis of fMRI responses in ventral temporal cortex.

✦

What it does

The notebook analyzes the Haxby fMRI dataset: 864 trials in which a subject viewed faces, houses, cats, bottles, scissors, shoes, chairs, and scrambled images. Responses in ventral temporal cortex have been reduced to 50 principal components. The central question is whether the brain represents these categories in distinguishable patterns.

It works in three stages. First it explores how mean component scores differ by category and between animate and inanimate objects. Next it runs a supervised decoding task, training classifiers to tell whether the subject was viewing a face. Finally it takes an unsupervised look at how the categories arrange themselves in representational space.

Logistic Regression

A linear classifier separating face from non-face trials. Across stratified 5-fold cross-validation it reached about 99% accuracy.

P(face) = σ(w·x + b)

k-Nearest Neighbors

Labels a trial by majority vote of its closest neighbors in standardized PC space. A sweep over k shows how neighborhood size affects accuracy. In cross-validation it scored about 98%, close behind the linear model.

ŷ = mode(y of k nearest)

PCA & t-SNE

Two views of the same 50-dimensional space. PCA keeps the global linear structure: its first two components explain about 52% of the variance. t-SNE draws out local clusters by category.

50 PCs → 2D map

What it shows

Faces are strikingly easy to decode. A simple linear model separates them from every other category almost perfectly, which fits the well-known face-selective responses in ventral temporal cortex. The animate/inanimate split also shows up in the average component profiles, suggesting that the brain's representational space is organized by broad semantic dimensions as well as by individual categories.
