Classic Optimization

✦

An exploration of the classic optimization methods that come before evolutionary computing: how you find the top of a hill when you can only feel the ground under your feet.

What it does

The playground is a Python notebook built with NumPy, Matplotlib, and SciPy. It sets up a series of surfaces and runs different optimizers across them to compare how each one searches.

It starts with the pumpkin problem: find the highest point of a launched pumpkin's arc, f(x) = 10 − (x − 5)². From there it adds a bumpy, multi-peaked surface where the starting point decides which peak you end up on, a learning-rate experiment that shows steps that are too small crawling and steps that are too large overshooting, and finally two-dimensional terrains such as (x² − 2)² − xy + (y² − 3)², which has more than one valley.

PythonNumPySciPyMatplotlibOptimizationJupyter

Guess & Check

The baseline. Evaluate the surface at a list of candidate points and keep the best one. It's simple, but it only finds what you happened to test.

x* = argmax f(xᵢ)

Gradient Ascent / Descent

Step in the direction the slope points, scaled by a learning rate. The notebook runs it from several starting points on the bumpy surface and in 2D.

x ← x + α·f′(x)  ·  v ← v − α·∇f(v)

Bisection

Looks for the peak by finding where the derivative crosses zero. Each step halves a bracket whose ends have slopes of opposite sign. It's slow, but it always converges.

f′(a)·f′(m) < 0 ⇒ b ← m, else a ← m

Newton's Method

Uses curvature as well as slope. On a quadratic it lands on the optimum in a single step. In 2D it solves against the Hessian.

x ← x − f′(x)/f″(x)  ·  v ← v − H⁻¹∇f

Nelder–Mead

A search that needs no derivatives. A triangle (simplex) reflects, expands, contracts, and shrinks its way downhill. There's both SciPy's version and a hand-written one that logs every step, with two starting points that settle into two different minima.

reflect → expand → contract → shrink

What the experiments show

Newton's method was the fastest on the smooth pumpkin surface. Gradient ascent is the workhorse of machine learning, but it depends on its learning rate and starting point. Bisection is the most dependable when all you can do is check which way the slope points.

On surfaces with several optima, every method here is local. The answer depends on where you begin. That limitation is what motivates the population-based, evolutionary approaches the course builds toward.
